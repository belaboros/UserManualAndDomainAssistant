# Structure pays most where models know least

Yes. Glossaries, taxonomies, ontologies and knowledge graphs help with all seven goals. How much they help, and how good the evidence is, varies from goal to goal. They help most where the language model knows least. For concepts that are new, contested or outside a user's home field, the model's built-in knowledge is stale or missing. It then either invents a plausible definition or follows whatever document it retrieved, so a curated, attributed concept layer becomes the arbiter of meaning. The strongest evidence is for fast onboarding (goal 3). Meta-analyses of concept maps show moderate learning gains (g≈0.43 for studying a map, g≈0.72 for building one). Next come cross-domain bridging (goal 2) and governed evolution (goal 6), where mature terminology infrastructure and versioning standards already exist. Support for new domains (goal 1) and conceptual answers (goal 5) is real but conditional. The structures must hold several dated, sourced definitions rather than one canonical truth. They must also be used for relational questions, not for simple definitional lookups. Deduction (goal 4) helps on a narrower band than intuition suggests. Structured querying lifted enterprise accuracy from 16% to 54%, but LLMs cannot be trusted to do the reasoning themselves. Inferences have to be computed by graph queries or reasoners and only *explained* by the LLM. Experimentation (goal 7) is not optional, because every benchmark shows the best stack flips by question class. No public benchmark covers ontology-backed, cross-domain or emerging-domain manual QA, so this project must build its own. All of this extends the architecture of the earlier report, *Knowledge graphs for domain assistants*. It keeps the deterministic structure spine, the seeded ontology, hybrid retrieval with graph context attached to source text, and a Reviewer agent that proposes but never commits. It adds definitions with full provenance, perspective-specific labels, typed cross-domain mappings, prerequisite edges, and a question router that keeps deduction out of the LLM.

## Seven verdicts, from strong yes to narrow yes

The table answers the question "do these structures help?" for each goal. The sections that follow give the reasoning. "Evidence strength" rates evidence that the *remedy* works, not evidence that the problem exists. For several goals the problem is well measured, while the structured fix has only been argued for or shown in a pilot.

| Goal | Verdict | Evidence strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Very new domains (AI, quantum) | **Yes, conditionally.** Structures act as the arbiter against stale or invented model knowledge, but only if definitions are plural, sourced, dated and given a status | Strong that the problem exists (neologisms, fabricated definitions, knowledge conflicts); weak for the remedy in manuals | Freezing a premature definition; one hype document dominating; unstable external ontologies |
| 2. Overlapping domains (medicine × CS) | **Strong yes for bridging concepts**, using typed, directional, confidence-scored mappings rather than equivalences | Strong infrastructure (UMLS, FHIR ConceptMap); LLM matching accuracy varies; no cross-discipline benchmark | False equivalences; mappings that are really analogies; over-trust in polished graph views |
| 3. Learning a new domain quickly | **Strong yes.** Glossary plus hierarchical map as "pre-training", graph views on demand, and active construction | Strong (meta-analyses, n>11,000) for concept maps; pilot-level for LLM tutors built on KGs | Dense network graphs overload novices; expertise reversal; visual over-trust |
| 4. Deductions beyond keyword or RAG search | **Narrow yes.** Large gains on structure-dependent questions, *if* inference is computed symbolically and verbalized by the LLM | Moderate (one strong vendor-affiliated benchmark; KGQA studies); no head-to-head LLM+reasoner vs RAG study | Incomplete graphs, closed-world misreading, brittle text-to-query (≈30–60% execution accuracy) |
| 5. Conceptual questions | **Split verdict.** "What is X": glossary yes, graph adds little. "How do X and Y relate / differ, what kinds of X": graph or hierarchy gains ≈8–13 points | Moderate (two GraphRAG benchmarks); self-reported gains elsewhere | Graph noise on simple lookups; structure-heavy retrievers falling below no-retrieval baselines |
| 6. Evolving glossary and ontology | **Yes, and it must be designed in from day one.** The standards are mature; automated evolution is research-stage | Strong for mechanisms (OWL, SKOS, OBO, PROV); weak for LLM-driven evolution | Silent redefinition breaks reproducibility of past answers; churn fatigue for reviewers |
| 7. Experimenting to find the right combination | **Essential and feasible**, using a factorial harness sliced by question class with paired statistics | Moderate to strong for methodology; weak for tool comparisons (vendor-run) | Judge style bias; synthetic test sets that rank generators wrongly; circular references |

## New domains: attributed definitions must overrule the model

The case for structure in very new domains starts with measured model failures. A single neologism in a sentence **nearly halves machine-translation quality** on NEO-BENCH, and models with later knowledge cutoffs do better ([arXiv 2402.12261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12261)). HypoTermQA built 790 validated non-existent terms, such as a fictitious "Wolf LLM", and showed that models readily fabricate explanations instead of admitting the term does not exist ([arXiv 2402.16211](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16211v1)). Knowledge conflicts make this worse in two opposite directions. Under context-memory conflict, models show "a marked tendency towards over-relying on parametric knowledge" ([Xu et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08319)), so an old meaning beats a renamed or redefined term in the manual. Yet on ClashEval, six top LLMs adopted *incorrect* retrieved content over a correct prior **more than 60% of the time**, and more often when their own confidence was low ([arXiv 2404.10198](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)). For a brand-new concept the model has no prior at all. It will follow whatever is retrieved, so the quality of curated sources matters most exactly where the concepts are newest. Retrieval itself clearly helps freshness ([FreshQA, arXiv 2310.03214](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03214)). The question is which retrieved definition wins.

Authoritative vocabularies cannot settle this, because they lag practice by years and then change. ISO/IEC 22989 (2022) only reached a draft amendment adding generative-AI terms in August 2025 ([DIN](https://www.din.de/en/wdc-beuth:din21:394126785)). ISO/IEC 4879:2024, the quantum computing vocabulary, had its revision validated at the November 2025 JTC 3 plenary, about 18 months after publication ([ITnation](https://itnation.lu/news/highlights-from-the-iec-iso-jtc-3-plenary-meeting-on-quantum-technology-standardization/)). There is still **no binding legal definition of "agentic AI"** in major statutes ([arXiv 2603.27075](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27075v1)). Machine-readable ontologies are just as young. The Artificial Intelligence Ontology moved from six top-level branches in its preprint to eight in its journal version. BioPortal still lists it as beta, and its repository has no tagged releases ([arXiv 2404.03044](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03044); [BioPortal](https://bioportal.bioontology.org/ontologies/AIO); [GitHub](https://github.com/berkeleybop/artificial-intelligence-ontology)). The pattern to copy comes from NIST itself. Its trustworthy-AI glossary deliberately keeps **multiple attributed meanings per term** rather than declaring one ([NIST AI 100-3](https://www.nist.gov/publications/language-trustworthy-ai-depth-glossary-terms)). Its adversarial-ML taxonomy is an explicitly versioned document, updated annually ([NIST AI 100-2 E2025](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ai/100/2/e2025/final)).

The verdict is therefore yes, but only for a structure built for disagreement. A concept carries 1..N definition records. Each records its text, source span, source type (standard, vendor manual, paper, internal), date, scope and status. The concept itself moves through a status lattice: candidate, proposed, accepted *or contested*, then deprecated. Term status reuses ISO 1087's preferred, admitted and deprecated labels ([ISO 1087 sample](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/62330/aa6fcc8f52e34cf39acee86fa098fc22/ISO-1087-2019.pdf)). A "marketing term" flag maps hype words onto the real concepts behind them. External AI and quantum ontologies are imported as pinned-version alignment targets through skos:exactMatch or skos:closeMatch, never as the backbone. The Reviewer assistant gains a new queue: candidate new terms that recur across incoming manuals but are absent from the glossary. Citation and burst signals detect emerging concepts well; King et al. report Precision@1000 of 99% on arXiv CS ([arXiv 2006.06877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06877)). Because HypoTermQA shows definitions are easily fabricated, no definition reaches "proposed" without a source span. The same benchmark design gives a go-live acceptance test: probe the assistant with invented terms and require "not in my sources". The honest gap is that no study measures RAG accuracy on post-cutoff concepts *in technical manuals*. The size of the benefit has to be measured on this project's own corpus.

## Evolution: concept IDs stay fixed and nothing is deleted

Goal 6 is where the standards are most mature. OWL 2 already provides version IRIs, owl:priorVersion, backwardCompatibleWith/incompatibleWith and owl:deprecated ([OWL 2](https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-syntax/)). SKOS allows multiple definitions and provides changeNote and historyNote for fine-grained and meaning-level change ([SKOS Primer](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-primer/)). PROV-O records which document or reviewer caused each revision ([PROV-O](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/)). The most battle-tested rule comes from the OBO Foundry. **A term whose meaning changes is obsoleted, not redefined in place.** It is marked deprecated, stripped of its logical axioms, and pointed to a replacement ([OBO Principle 19](https://obofoundry.org/principles/fp-019-term-stability.html)). Even that community was still tightening its obsoletion policy in 2024–25 ([OBO newsletter](https://obofoundry.org/newsletter/2025/01/23/7th-issue-newsletter.html)). For property graphs, Graphiti gives edges validity windows and invalidates contradicted facts instead of deleting them ([vectorize.io](https://vectorize.io/articles/zep-vs-cognee)). That is a ready model for "this was true until release 3.2".

What is missing is automation and a standard for contested status. Surveys have long found that "a complete integrated system for automated ontology evolution is not available yet" ([Leipzig survey listing](https://pubs.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/se/node/985)). LLM approaches such as Evo-DKD, which proposes edits with justifications, are prototypes ([arXiv 2507.21438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21438)). TRACE-OU-Bench argues that schema change is harder than ordinary temporal drift, and workshop authors call for hybrid automated-plus-human evolution ([CEUR Vol-4316](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4316/pd-short-8.pdf); [CEUR Vol-4020](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4020/Paper_ID_13.pdf)). That is exactly the Reviewer-agent loop from the earlier report. The new requirement for evolving domains is *reproducibility*. Every answer should record the glossary and ontology release it used, so "why does the assistant say something different now?" can be answered from the change log. Two design proposals are not drawn from any standard. Releases can be semantically versioned: patch for wording, minor for additions and deprecations, major for re-parenting or removed axioms. The assistant can also show historyNote-style messages to users ("until v3 this was called X") whenever a meaning-level change touches their question.

## Overlap and onboarding: shared concepts with labels for each field, trees first

Goals 2 and 3 share one mechanism. A person who is an expert at home but a novice in the target field has trouble because they do not know the concepts and the relations between them. Learning science has measured remedies for exactly this. Schroeder et al.'s meta-analysis pools 142 effect sizes (n=11,814) and finds concept maps improve learning with **g=0.58 overall: g=0.72 when learners build the map and g=0.43 when they study a ready-made one** ([Schroeder et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)). Map structure matters. Learners with both low and high prior knowledge spent **less mental effort on hierarchical maps than on network maps** ([Amadieu et al. 2009](https://research.ou.nl/ws/files/1022307/Amadieu-etal_LI_2009.pdf)). A 2025 study with 136 students showed that a concept map plus a glossary used as "pre-training" reduced extraneous cognitive load, and low-prior-knowledge learners benefited most ([Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1628047/full)). The counterweight is expertise reversal. Worked-out maps that help novices can become redundant for experienced learners ([Hilbert & Renkl 2009](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2009-hilbert.pdf)). Scaffolding should therefore depend on how well the user knows *each concept*, not on the user as a whole. An engineer is an expert in "classification" but a novice in "differential diagnosis".

For overlapping fields, the right conceptual model is the *boundary object*. Star and Griesemer's term describes an artifact that lets communities coordinate without full consensus ([Boundary object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_object)). The Gene Ontology was founded explicitly so that separate groups could share "the same language" ([GO Consortium](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC311077)). UMLS shows the engineering version at scale: about **3.49M concepts from 190 source vocabularies**, each source keeping its own names ([NLM](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/research/umls/knowledge_sources/metathesaurus/release/statistics.html)). FHIR ConceptMap $translate standardizes directional mappings ([HL7 FHIR](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/terminology-service.html)). The evidence on automating such links is mixed. Zero-shot LLM matching reached **89 F1 on Anatomy but only 26 F1 on SNOMED–FMA**, against 78.5 for the best classical system ([LLMs4OM, arXiv 2404.10317](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10317v1)). In OAEI 2025, LogMap with an LLM *oracle consulted only for uncertain pairs* ranked in the top two of the Bio-ML track ([arXiv 2508.08500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08500v2)). Hallucinated mappings are a catalogued failure mode ([OAEI-LLM, arXiv 2409.14038](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14038)). No benchmark exists for mapping *between disciplines*, for example medicine to computer science. Such links are often analogies rather than equivalences. "Sensitivity" is exactly "recall". "Differential diagnosis" only resembles ranked classification. "Model", "protocol" and "agent" mean different things on each side.

The design follows from this evidence. Each concept carries a stable ID with labels and definitions specific to each field. Cross-field links are typed exact, close, related or *analogy*. Each link records where the analogy breaks down, its confidence and provenance, and whether a human has curated it. LLM-proposed links are visually distinct from curated ones. A cross-domain answer has four parts: a one-line definition in target-field terms, a labelled bridge to the nearest concept in the user's home field, a small local subgraph, and source passages. Evidence that adapting explanations to expertise helps is positive but small. One study raised understanding, but its classifier matched explanations to the right audience only about 50–62% of the time ([arXiv 2604.21092](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21092)). LLM-generated analogies have been shown to help *models*, not human learners ([arXiv 2406.11375](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11375v1)). So analogies must be labelled as analogies.

The legal onboarding example illustrates both promise and limits. Reusable assets exist: LKIF Core's role, action and process modules, and FOLIO's more than 18,000 CC-BY OWL concepts ([LKIF Core](https://github.com/RinkeHoekstra/lkif-core); [FOLIO](https://github.com/alea-institute/folio)). No maintained court-trial process ontology was found, however, and roles differ substantially between common-law and civil-law systems. Civil-law judges investigate and question witnesses, while common-law judges preside over adversarial advocates ([US DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-ovt/roles-civil-legal-systems); [FJC](https://judiciariesworldwide.fjc.gov/civil-common-law)). A "taxonomy of lawyers and judges" must therefore be parameterized by jurisdiction. The trial graph (filing, pre-trial, hearing, judgment, appeal, linked to actors) is custom modelling that needs domain-expert review. The main onboarding risk is visual over-trust. In LinkQ, 14 practitioners, including KG experts, trusted wrong answers *because* the graph visualizations looked helpful ([arXiv 2505.21512](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.21512)). No controlled study yet compares an LLM assistant with and without a concept map on how fast adult newcomers come to understand a domain. Running one would be a genuine contribution.

## Deduction and conceptual answers: the graph computes, the LLM explains

Goal 4 is where intuition most overstates the benefit, so the boundary needs to be precise. When questions depend on schema, structure clearly wins. On insurance-enterprise questions, GPT-4 scored **16% over SQL and 54% over a KG representation**, and on high-schema-complexity questions it went from 0% to roughly 36–39% ([Sequeda et al., arXiv 2311.07509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07509)). This is vendor-affiliated but published. Free-form query generation is the weak link. Text-to-SPARQL reaches only about **49–59% execution accuracy** on public benchmarks, versus about 30% for a vanilla LLM ([arXiv 2508.01815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.01815)), and Cypher accuracy falls as query complexity rises ([CypherBench](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2412.18702v2)). Asking the LLM to reason over the ontology itself fails more sharply. The best LLM's is-a accuracy drops by **up to 30%** from common to specialised domains and from root to leaf concepts ([TaxoGlimpse, VLDB 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11131v1)). GPT-4o handled disjointness transitivity poorly, inferred nothing on large instance sets, and answered **no LUBM query fully correctly** ([arXiv 2406.17532](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17532v2)). No controlled study shows an LLM plus OWL reasoner beating RAG on QA accuracy. The reasoner's documented value is validation, for example HermiT feeding inconsistency explanations back to the LLM ([arXiv 2504.07640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07640)), and materializing entailments offline.

The answer for goal 4 is therefore a narrow yes with a fixed division of labour. The reasoner materializes subclass closure, transitive prerequisites and inverse relations offline. Parameterized, expert-reviewed query templates answer "what kinds of X", "what is needed before X" and "how does X relate to Y". Free-form Text2Cypher or Text2SPARQL is only a fallback, validated and repaired against the schema ([arXiv 2405.11706](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11706v1)). The LLM plans the query and explains the result; it never performs the deduction. Two failure modes need explicit handling. KG-augmented methods degrade when key facts are missing and quietly fall back on memorised knowledge ([arXiv 2504.05163](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05163v2); [BRINK](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08344v4)). OWL is open-world, so a missing edge must never come out as "no". Every derived claim should be labelled "stated in manual §3.2" or "inferred by inheritance from Y". Answers that rest only on the graph should fall back to the manual passages and say what is missing.

For goal 5, the type of conceptual question decides the verdict. On a 1,018-question CS-textbook benchmark, gains over a no-retrieval baseline (74.3%) were small: RAPTOR scored 76.7%. Structure-heavy methods such as LightRAG (65.2%) fell *below* that baseline, because retrieval noise overrode answers the model already knew. Retrieval did improve *reasoning soundness*: accuracy with a sound rationale rose from 39.8 to 45.5 ([Xiao et al., arXiv 2506.02404](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02404v2)). The ICLR 2026 GraphRAG-Bench draws the routing line more sharply:

| Method (novel corpus, GPT-4o-mini) | Fact retrieval | Complex reasoning | Contextual summarization |
|---|---|---|---|
| RAG + rerank | **60.9** | 42.9 | 51.3 |
| MS-GraphRAG | 49.3 | 50.9 | **64.4** |
| HippoRAG2 | 60.1 | **53.4** | 64.1 |

Source: [Xiang et al., arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690v3). "What is X" behaves like fact retrieval: a curated glossary entry with its parent class and one or two manual passages is enough, and a graph adds noise. Relational, comparative and enumerative questions ("how does X differ from Y", "what kinds of X") behave like complex reasoning or summarization, where graph or hierarchy methods gain about 8–13 points. HippoRAG2 achieves this at roughly vector-RAG token cost (about 1,008 against 879 tokens per query), while MS-GraphRAG local search uses about 38,700. OG-RAG reports **+40% response correctness and 30% faster attribution by users** from ontology-grounded fact clusters, though only on agriculture and news corpora ([arXiv 2412.15235](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15235v1)). RAPTOR's success on textbooks also points to a cheap signal already in the manuals: their chapter and section hierarchy. Displayed reasoning paths should be designed to calibrate trust, not to maximise it. Plausible paths help users, implausible ones raise doubt ([GradPath, ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.654.pdf)), and polished visualizations caused over-trust in LinkQ.

## Experimentation: a factorial harness sliced by question class

Goal 7 is a firm yes, because the evidence makes one point unavoidable. No stack wins overall. RAG beats graphs on fact lookup, graphs beat RAG on reasoning and summarization, and in aviation maintenance a KG "markedly improves global sensemaking" while chunk RAG stays better for procedures ([KEO, arXiv 2510.05524](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05524v1)). Averaged metrics hide this crossover, so every metric must be broken down by question class. The earlier report's evaluation harness (gold set with underspecified, unanswerable and conflicting questions; around 100 labelled flags per detector type) should gain four classes for these goals. Cross-domain questions should include term collisions. Emerging-domain questions should use terms newer than the model's cutoff or missing from the ontology. Relational and comparative conceptual questions should be a class of their own. Fabricated-term probes should test abstention. The ontology also gives free gold answers for structural questions. Competency-question templates are instantiated per entity, and the answer is computed from the KG. Pitfall-oriented competency-question generation targets gaps such as missing disjointness ([VSPO, arXiv 2511.07991](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07991v2)).

Statistical discipline matters more than tool choice. Synthetic questions rank *retriever* configurations reliably but **fail to rank generator architectures** ([arXiv 2508.11758](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.11758)). Generator and model comparisons therefore belong on a human gold set of a few hundred items. Prediction-powered inference turns a few hundred human labels plus many judge labels into valid confidence intervals ([ARES, arXiv 2311.09476](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09476v2)). Comparisons should use paired, question-level differences with standard errors and power analysis ([Miller, arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640?context=cs)). LLM judges now show little position bias but large style bias, 0.76–0.92 in one single-author preprint ([arXiv 2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23178)). Assertion-based scoring, rubrics, swapped orderings, a judge from a different model family, and repeated trials (Microsoft used six) reduce this ([BenchmarkQED](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1140429)). A fine-tuned 400M-parameter DeBERTa evaluator outperformed few-shot LLM judges on RAGBench, which includes manual-like datasets ([arXiv 2407.11005](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11005v2)). The knowledge structures need their own quality scorecard, so that ablations can be interpreted. Sampled triple accuracy cuts annotation cost by 60–80% ([Gao et al., arXiv 1907.09657](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.09657v1)). Add reasoner and SHACL consistency checks, OOPS! pitfall counts, and the share of competency questions answered correctly.

| Phase | What varies | Data and metrics | Decision it informs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Baselines | No retrieval; BM25; dense; hybrid + reranker | Human gold set, per class | How much each layer adds over the model alone |
| 1. Factor screening | Glossary expansion and definition injection; taxonomy routing; KG retrieval (templates, neighbourhood, Text2Cypher); GraphRAG engine (LazyGraphRAG, LightRAG, HippoRAG2); materialized entailments; agentic router | Large synthetic + competency-question set; evidence recall and context precision per class; tokens/query | Fractional-factorial or leave-one-out ablation from the full stack; watch glossary × KG interactions |
| 2. End-to-end | Top 3–5 configurations + baselines | Human gold, ≥3 samples/question, swap-checked judge, assertions, PPI; correctness, citation accuracy, abstention F1, terminology correctness, $/query, p95 latency | Pareto choice of quality vs cost, likely *routing by question class* |
| 3. Robustness | Emerging-domain holdout added incrementally; cross-domain collision set; paraphrases; KG degraded by 10/20%; fabricated terms | Same metrics + update cost | How much KG quality matters; whether incremental indexing keeps up with domain churn |
| 4. Human outcomes | Onboarding with vs without glossary pre-training and hierarchical map; analogy bridges on vs off | Time to criterion on a concept quiz, error rate, trust calibration | The goal-3 claim no published study has tested with LLM assistants |
| 5. Production | Frozen regression split in CI; shadow mode; A/B test | Thumbs, escalation rate, time to resolution, citation clicks | Release gating; new gold items from real questions |

Build the harness around one retriever interface that returns passages with provenance. Plug BM25, dense, hybrid, glossary lookup, Cypher templates, SPARQL over a reasoner-materialized store, and each GraphRAG engine in behind it. Every run should equal one configuration hash. The GraphRAG-Bench repository already wraps several engines against vanilla RAG ([arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05690)). MedRAG's factorial design found that fusing retrievers beats any single retriever ([arXiv 2402.13178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13178)). Public proxies are useful only for validating the harness, not for decisions. They include TechQA, EManual and DelucionQA for manuals, GraphRAG-Bench Medical for structured domain text, and MIRAGE combined with a CS corpus as a synthetic medicine × CS stress test. Vendor leaderboards, such as FalkorDB's claimed 66 vs 48 on GraphRAG-Bench ([FalkorDB](https://docs.falkordb.com/graphrag/benchmark)), should be re-run before anyone believes them.

## Additions to the earlier architecture

The earlier report's blueprint stays intact: deterministic structure spine, seeded schema, LLM proposals, SHACL gates, hybrid retrieval with graph context, issue store, and a Reviewer that never commits. The seven goals add the following layers.

| Layer | Addition | Driven by |
|---|---|---|
| Concept model | Definition records (text, span, source type, date, scope, status); concept lattice candidate → proposed → accepted/contested → deprecated; ISO 1087 term status; hype flag | Goals 1, 6 (NIST AI 100-3; HypoTermQA; ClashEval) |
| Versioning | Opaque IDs; obsolete-and-replace per OBO P19; owl:versionIRI per release; validity windows on edges; each answer stamped with the vocabulary release it used | Goal 6 (OWL 2, OBO, Graphiti) |
| Cross-domain layer | Labels and definitions per field on a shared concept ID; typed mappings exact/close/related/analogy with breakdown notes; medical side anchored to UMLS/SNOMED IDs; matcher + LLM-oracle proposals into the review queue | Goal 2 (UMLS, LogMap-LLM, OAEI-LLM) |
| Learner layer | User profile with home field, target field, jurisdiction and per-concept familiarity; prerequisite edges (LLM-proposed, human-validated); tree view first, 1-hop ego graph on click, full graph on request; "place this role" construction tasks | Goal 3 (Schroeder; Amadieu; expertise reversal) |
| Question router | "What is X" → glossary + parent + passages; "kinds of X" / "needed before X" / "X vs Y" / "X relates to Y" → templates over materialized closure, then LLM verbalization; free text-to-query only with validation and repair; open-world-aware answer labels | Goals 4, 5 (TaxoGlimpse; DL-Lite; GraphRAG-Bench) |
| Reviewer queues | New-term candidates by burst and citation signals; definition-drift and definition-conflict items; cross-domain mapping proposals; prerequisite proposals; contested-concept adjudication | Goals 1, 2, 6 (King et al.; hybrid-evolution consensus) |
| Acceptance tests | Fabricated-term probes; competency-question regression suite; cross-domain collision set; per-class thresholds in CI | Goals 1, 7 |

## Conclusion

The earlier report concluded that the knowledge graph is mainly a verification substrate rather than a better retriever. These seven goals extend that conclusion. In emerging and overlapping domains, the structures also become the *authority on meaning* and a *map for learning*. These are roles that retrieval quality cannot fill, because the model's own knowledge is weakest exactly there. This reverses the usual cost-benefit argument. For mature, single-domain manuals a well-tuned hybrid RAG gets close to expert behaviour, and the graph adds a few points. For AI, quantum computing, or medicine as seen by an engineer, the curated concept layer decides whether the assistant gives the user's field's meaning or a confident invention. The value comes less from the graph's reasoning power than from three properties: the layer can hold several attributed definitions, keep a meaning's history, and translate between communities without forcing agreement.

Two implications follow. First, the domain admin's job shifts from building a model of a domain to curating a living, contested vocabulary. The Reviewer agent's most valuable queues will be new-term detection, definition conflicts and cross-field mappings, not triple corrections. Second, two of this project's experiments would be novel results in their own right. One is a controlled test of whether concept maps and labelled analogies speed up adult newcomers using an LLM assistant. The other is a benchmark of RAG versus ontology-routed answering on post-cutoff concepts in manuals. Neither exists in the literature, and both could decide which goals justify their engineering cost.
