# Layperson Explanation UX: Glossary/Ontology-Backed Assistant for Understanding an Official Inheritance Letter

Scope: a person whose father died receives an official inheritance letter (notary, court or lawyer). The assistant answers "who is who / what is my role / what must I do by when", shows term definitions, an ontology diagram and a taxonomy diagram of the concepts in the letter, and suggests related terms. Jurisdiction-agnostic. Research cut-off: November 2026. Each finding below is marked as **[Evidence]** (measured, peer-reviewed or regulatory fact), **[Claim]** (author or vendor assertion, case study or preprint without independent replication), or **[Practice]** (practitioner or design guidance).

## 1. Document-grounded pipeline: term extraction, entity linking, party/role/deadline/obligation extraction, LLM accuracy

### Takeaway
The pipeline has three parts: extract terms and entities, link them to a glossary or ontology, then pull out parties, obligations and deadlines. Each part is technically feasible, but published accuracy on legal NER and entity linking is still mediocre and varies a lot by language and jurisdiction. I found no benchmark for "role/obligation extraction from official notices to laypeople". The product should therefore treat extraction as a draft that the user can check against the source text, not as ground truth.

### Cited Findings
- [Evidence] LegalBench (Guha et al., NeurIPS 2023) is a benchmark of 162 tasks written by legal professionals. It covers six reasoning types and evaluates 20 open-source and commercial LLMs. The six types are reported as issue/problem identification, rule recall, rule application, rule conclusion, interpretation and rhetorical understanding. Definition-related tasks appear as their own appendix section. — [arXiv 2308.11462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462v1); [NeurIPS listing](https://mlanthology.org/neurips/2023/guha2023neurips-legalbench)
- [Evidence] LegalBench turns CUAD contract-clause span extraction into 38 binary classification tasks, such as non-compete, IP ownership, termination and exclusivity. I found no party-identification or "role of recipient" task. The original CUAD covers 41 clause types. — [LegalBench wiki summary](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/legalbench); [CUAD paper](https://ar5iv.org/html/2103.06268)
- [Evidence, secondary] GPT-4 reportedly averaged only 47.8% balanced accuracy on the MAUD (merger agreement) tasks in LegalBench. Some fine-grained legal reading tasks therefore remain hard even for frontier models of that generation. — [LegalBench wiki summary](https://www.aiwiki.ai/wiki/legalbench/raw) (secondary; verify against the paper's per-task tables)
- [Evidence] A comparative study of LLMs for legal NER (IEEE BigData 2024, Lamarr Institute) found "significant variability in LLM performance across different languages and legal contexts". GPT-4 scored highest overall. — [Lamarr Institute](https://lamarr-institute.org/publication/a-comparative-study-of-large-language-models-for-named-entity-recognition-in-the-legal-domain/)
- [Evidence] A 2025 tax-law system that combined a co-developed ontology with a custom NER model reached only about 53% F-measure on specialised legal entities, while still beating baselines. Linking to domain-specific concepts is much harder than generic NER (person, organisation, date). — [VJIKMS 2025 via CiteDrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/semantic-legal-information-retrieval-for-tax-law-using-ai-and-ontology-based-methods/)
- [Evidence/Claim] Older but foundational work links recognised legal entities to the LKIF core legal ontology and studies how to populate legal ontologies with few annotations. — [ICAIL, Teruel et al.](https://cs.famaf.unc.edu.ar/~mteruel/papers/[ICAIL]_A_low-cost,_high-coverage_legal_NERC.pdf)
- [Claim] LLM-based structured extraction "often fail[s] to handle long documents effectively". LINK-KG adds LLM-guided coreference resolution to build entity-linked knowledge graphs from case texts. This matters for letters where the same person appears as "the deceased", "the testator" and a proper name. — [arXiv 2510.26486](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26486v1)
- [Claim, preprint] Ontology-driven Graph RAG for legal norms: an LRMoo-inspired ontology separates abstract legal works from versioned expressions, giving a "temporally-correct substrate" for LLMs. It is validated only by a case study on the Brazilian Constitution. — [arXiv 2505.00039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00039v5)
- [Claim, preprint] HalluGraph checks legal RAG output by aligning knowledge graphs. It scores entity grounding and relation preservation against the source and reports AUC 0.979 on structured documents and about 0.89 on a harder generative legal task. It degrades on short, sparse contexts. — [arXiv 2512.01659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01659)
- [Evidence] General legal hallucination rates are high when answers are not grounded. Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun and Ho ("Large Legal Fictions", Journal of Legal Analysis 2024) found hallucination rates of 69–88% on verifiable US case-law queries for GPT-3.5, Llama 2 and PaLM 2. — [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/hallucinating-law-legal-mistakes-large-language-models-are-pervasive); [arXiv 2401.01301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01301)
- [Evidence, secondary report] The follow-up "Hallucination-Free?" study (Magesh et al.) reportedly found that even RAG legal research tools hallucinated: Lexis+ AI about 17% and Westlaw AI-Assisted Research about 33%, against GPT-4 at about 43%. — [LLRX summary, 2026](https://www.llrx.com/2026/02/what-the-science-says-about-hallucinations-in-legal-research/) (secondary; I did not check these figures against the original paper)

### Inferences
- A practical architecture: (1) parse the letter into structured fields: sender and authority type, case or file number, deceased, addressees and their stated capacity, requested actions, deadlines, attachments, and legal bases cited. (2) Match terms against a curated glossary or ontology first, with the LLM as fallback, so definitions come from vetted entries rather than being generated. (3) Show every extracted fact with a highlight back to the exact span in the letter (span-level provenance), so the user and any reviewer can check it. (4) Run a grounding check, HalluGraph-style or simpler string or entailment checks, before showing role or deadline claims.
- The source letter is short and self-contained, which makes the extraction task easier than open legal QA. Most hallucination risk moves to two places: inferring things the letter does not say (for example "you must pay inheritance tax") and jurisdiction-specific general knowledge. The UI should visibly separate "stated in your letter" from "generally true in inheritance processes" from "depends on your jurisdiction, ask the notary".
- Deadlines are the highest-stakes field. Date normalisation (relative deadlines such as "within 30 days of receipt", or local date formats) should be deterministic code, not left to the LLM, and should always show the original wording.
- Entity linking to a jurisdiction-agnostic ontology needs a two-layer model. Generic concepts (estate, heir, testament, executor, acceptance or renunciation of inheritance, probate or succession certificate, debts of the estate, forced share) map to local legal terms (for example "Erbschein", "acte de notoriété", "grant of probate"). Cross-jurisdiction concept mapping of this kind already exists for practitioners in the EU (see EU Adapt in section 4).

### Gaps
- No public benchmark or accuracy figures were found for extracting addressee role, obligations and deadlines from official letters (notices, summonses, notary letters), as distinct from contracts.
- I did not verify the per-task LegalBench numbers for the definition-extraction or "who is the party" tasks, or the RAGE-KG 2025 graph-RAG comparison results (the [CEUR paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4079/paper6.pdf) snippet had no numbers).
- I found no peer-reviewed independent replication showing that KG or ontology grounding reduces legal hallucination for consumer QA. The evidence is preprints with self-reported metrics.

## 2. Personalization: identifying the user's role, "what this means for you", next steps, email-specific subgraph, related-concept expansion

### Takeaway
The best evidence for "what this means for you" design comes from behavioural redesigns of official notices, not from AI. Making the action, date and place salient sharply improved compliance, and people who failed to act were mostly unaware rather than unwilling. Users of legal AI say they want a navigator (issue spotting, next steps, referrals) rather than a lawyer substitute. I found no empirical study of subgraph diagrams or "related concepts" suggestions for laypeople, so those features are untested design hypotheses.

### Cited Findings
- [Evidence] Fishbane, Ouss and Shah (Science, 2020) evaluated New York City's redesigned court summons. The redesign moved the date and time to the top, clarified the location and labelled the form as a court appearance ticket. It cut failures to appear by about 13%. NYC's own evaluation reported a 6.4 percentage-point drop from a 41% baseline. Text-message reminders cut failures to appear by about 21%. Lab experiments showed that the redesign improved identification and recall of the key court information, and the authors conclude that most no-shows are unintentional. — [Science paper PDF (ideas42)](https://www.ideas42.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Behavioral-nudges-reduce-failure-to-appear-for-court_Science.full_.pdf); [J-PAL evaluation](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/2395); [Chicago Booth release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/anuj-shah-research-nyc-court-attendance)
- [Evidence/Practice] In Stanford Legal Design Lab rubric research, users wanted AI to act "like a navigation system": spotting issues, finding next steps and reaching the right help. The Lab proposes judging answers on whether they are actionable, whether they build empowerment, and whether they avoid needless fear or discouragement, rather than on how well they imitate a lawyer. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab, benchmarks tag](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/tag/benchmarks/)
- [Evidence/Practice] The Lab's legal-aid quality benchmarks include step-by-step next steps, jurisdiction-specific information, referrals to real resources and plain language. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab, legal AI tag](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/tag/legal-ai)
- [Evidence, anecdotal] In a Missouri intake tool built with Suffolk LIT Lab, the model sometimes asked for information the user had already given, which worsened the experience and delayed decisions. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/category/current-projects/page/2/)
- [Claim] An August 2026 Stanford/Los Angeles Superior Court trial will test AI error checks, legal-help referrals and redesigned notices in tenant cases. Results were not yet available. — [Stanford News, Aug 2026](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/08/ai-tools-tenant-cases-los-angeles-superior-court)
- [Claim] JusticeBot (Cyberjustice Lab, Montréal) uses a "augmented intelligence" methodology: a hand-built legal pathway graph of questions leading to legal information. It is not free-text generation. It has been embedded on the Quebec housing tribunal's website since July 2020. — [Westermann & Benyekhlef, arXiv 2308.02032](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.02032)

### Inferences
- **Role identification.** Combine (a) the letter's salutation and the capacity it states ("as legal heir", "as named executor", "as creditor"), (b) relationship facts the user gives ("I'm the son"), and (c) ontology rules (child → statutory heir in most systems, subject to the will). Always show the role with its basis and confidence, for example "The letter addresses you as heir (line 3)", and let the user correct it. Avoid asking again for anything the letter already answers (lesson from the Missouri intake tool).
- **"What this means for you" card**, modelled on the NYC summons redesign: put the action, the deadline (with countdown and calendar export), where and how to respond, and the consequence of not responding at the top. Supporting detail comes after. Add reminder nudges, given the roughly 21% effect of text reminders on failure to appear.
- **Email-specific subgraph.** Instantiate an ontology subgraph containing only the nodes evidenced in the letter (deceased, user, notary or court, other heirs, estate, will, deadline, requested document), with edges such as `addresses`, `heir_of`, `administers`, `requires`, `due_by`. Then add a second, visually distinct ring of "likely next" concepts. These come from a process-stage model of the inheritance workflow, for example notification → acceptance/renunciation decision → inventory and debts → certificate or probate → tax → distribution. Prerequisite edges in the ontology (`requires`, `precedes`, `alternative_to`) drive the "related terms you'll need" list. This mirrors JusticeBot's curated pathway approach, which keeps suggestions auditable.
- **Taxonomy view.** Use an is-a hierarchy (e.g. Person role → Heir → Statutory heir / Testamentary heir; Document → Will → Holographic / Notarial will). It is probably best offered as an "explore" panel, not as the primary answer. There is no evidence that laypeople benefit from taxonomies as a first view.

### Gaps
- I found no user study on whether layperson-facing knowledge-graph or ontology diagrams improve comprehension or task success. This should be tested (e.g. A/B of text answer vs text + subgraph).
- I found no published end-user comprehension or outcome evaluation of JusticeBot. Reported usage is "thousands" of users, and a Stanford page cites more than 35k uses with no date.

## 3. Plain-language legal explanation and visual law: evidence on comprehension and risks

### Takeaway
LLM simplification yields real but small comprehension gains on legal text: +3.5 points in the largest randomized study, the smallest of any domain tested. There is a fidelity tension: simplification can silently drop legally operative content, and laypeople tend to favour LLM advice even when they can tell it apart from a lawyer's. Visual law has controlled-experiment support: diagrams improved the speed and accuracy of comprehension. That evidence comes mostly from one research group, with experts or civil servants as participants.

### Cited Findings
- [Evidence, preprint] Guidroz et al. (2025, Google; arXiv 2505.01980) ran a randomized study with 4,563 participants and 31 texts across 6 domains. LLM "minimally lossy" simplification raised multiple-choice comprehension by 3.9 points overall (p<0.05). The gains were: biomedical 14.6, finance 5.5, aerospace/CS 3.8, **legal 3.5**. The effect held whether or not readers could refer back to the text. Perceived ease improved by 0.33 on a 5-point simplified NASA-TLX scale. Fidelity and omission errors are not reported on the abstract page. — [arXiv 2505.01980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980)
- [Evidence, medical analogue] In a crowdsourced lay study, readers rated LLM plain-language summaries similarly to human-written ones, but comprehension tests favoured the human-written summaries. Background knowledge strongly predicted understanding. Satisfaction ratings therefore overstate actual comprehension. — [arXiv 2505.10409 (Emergent Mind)](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2505.10409)
- [Evidence] Schneiders et al., "Objection Overruled!" (CHI 2025; 3 experiments, N=288): laypeople were more willing to act on LLM-generated legal advice than on lawyer advice when the source was hidden, even though they could partly tell the two apart. The authors flag over-reliance as a risk. — [arXiv 2409.07871](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07871v2); [Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/494609/)
- [Claim/Analysis] A legal-linguistics analysis distinguishes summarisation, which may omit or alter details, from simplification, which should keep the text's legal function (for example the binding force of definitions). It finds current tools produce summaries but not true functional reformulation. — [Journal of Language and Law](https://languageandlaw.eu/jll/article/download/208/105/836)
- [Claim] A Utrecht master's thesis found that Claude Sonnet 4 and GPT-4o produced B1-level Dutch legal simplifications that "largely" kept legal meaning, judged by automatic metrics, expert review and LLM-as-judge. There was no lay comprehension test. — [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/50462?show=full)
- [Claim] LegalEase (2025) is a synthetic LLM-generated dataset for lay legal summarisation. The authors state that no prior studies had examined LLMs for making legal documents accessible to the public. — [Springer AI Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11392-7)
- [Claim] LLMs can produce accessible explanations of a rule-based (Prolog) legal reasoner's outputs. This is a hybrid "explainable law" design, shown as a case study without user testing. — [arXiv 2311.11811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11811)
- [Evidence] Passera, Kankaanranta and Louhiala-Salminen ran an experiment with 122 contract professionals from 24 countries. Adding diagrams to a contract led to faster and more accurate comprehension for native and non-native English speakers. Legal background and cognitive style did not moderate the effect. — [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/diagrams-in-contracts-fostering-understanding-in-global-business-/)
- [Evidence] Passera (2018, Journal of Business and Technical Communication) found that flowcharts, swimlanes and timelines for legal-bureaucratic instructions improved comprehension accuracy and speed compared with prose, with civil servants as participants. The author notes the field still needs "more rigorously collected" empirical evidence. — [JBTC submission PDF](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/9920876/JBTC_submission.pdf)
- [Claim] Passera and Haapio's "The Quest for Clarity" introduces contract visualization patterns (timelines, flowcharts, icons, layered text). — [IGI chapter](https://irma-international.org/chapter/the-quest-for-clarity/78720)
- [Evidence] In the NYC summons redesign (Science 2020, section 2), visual hierarchy alone improved recall and real-world behaviour. This is the strongest field evidence that legal-document design changes outcomes for laypeople. — [Science PDF](https://www.ideas42.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Behavioral-nudges-reduce-failure-to-appear-for-court_Science.full_.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use **layered explanation**: (1) a one-line plain-language summary, (2) a "what you must do" card, (3) a paragraph-by-paragraph plain rewrite aligned side by side with the original, (4) term definitions on hover or tap from the curated glossary, (5) the original text, always reachable. Alignment to the original limits hidden omissions, the core risk raised by the Journal of Language and Law analysis.
- Add an **omission check**: compare the obligations, deadlines and conditions extracted from the original against those present in the simplification, and flag any missing. This is a direct mitigation for the summarisation-vs-simplification fidelity gap.
- Because laypeople over-trust LLM advice (Schneiders et al.) and satisfaction exceeds comprehension (medical analogue), avoid confident advisory phrasing on decisions such as accepting or renouncing an inheritance. Present options with consequences and refer to the notary or lawyer. Consider comprehension-check micro-questions ("Do you know your deadline?") rather than relying on thumbs-up feedback.
- Recommended visuals: **timeline** (process stages plus letter deadlines; the Passera evidence covers timelines and flowcharts), **role/swimlane diagram** (who does what: notary, heirs, executor, court, tax office), and **who-is-who relationship diagram** (family and role graph). A full ontology diagram is closer to an expert artefact. It should probably be simplified to a focused subgraph of 7±2 nodes.

### Gaps
- The visual-law comprehension evidence is mostly from Passera's group, with professional or civil-servant participants. I found no randomized study of visualised inheritance or probate documents with bereaved laypeople.
- I found no study measuring the rate of legally material omissions in LLM simplifications of official letters, or whether laypeople notice them.
- I did not locate a CHI or JURIX user study testing interactive glossary or ontology diagrams for legal documents.

## 4. Existing consumer/citizen legal AI products and pilots: what they learned

### Takeaway
Successful citizen legal tools tend to be scoped, curated and paired with a referral path: JusticeBot's structured pathways, Beagle+ with expert rubrics, legal-aid chatbots with human oversight. Regulators penalise "AI lawyer" overclaiming (the FTC's DoNotPay order). In inheritance specifically, notary and EU portals are human-maintained information databases. I found no notary-run AI assistant, which leaves a gap and a partnership opportunity.

### Cited Findings
- [Evidence] FTC v. DoNotPay: the complaint (Sept 2024) said the "robot lawyer" failed to live up to claims of substituting for a human lawyer and was never tested to that standard. The final order (Commission vote 5-0, January 2025; publicised February 2025) requires $193,000 in monetary relief, notice to 2021–2023 subscribers, and a ban on claiming it performs like a lawyer without evidence. — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87474); [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2296777)
- [Evidence/Claim] JusticeBot has been on the official site of Quebec's Tribunal administratif du logement since July 2020. It is built by legal experts encoding question pathways, with a consumer and privacy version announced in 2023. Usage is reported as thousands of users, or more than 35k uses per a Stanford page (undated). I found no published user-outcome evaluation. — [arXiv 2308.02032](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.02032v1.pdf); [Cyberjustice Lab 2023](https://cyberjustice.openum.ca/en/2023/09/14/un-robot-repondra-a-vos-questions-en-matiere-de-consommation-et-de-vie-privee/); [Stanford Legal Design Lab](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/category/current-projects/ai-a2j/page/4)
- [Practice] Beagle+ (People's Law School, British Columbia; launched early 2024) adopted the Stanford Legal Q&A evaluation rubrics to benchmark its user-facing chatbot. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/tag/benchmarks/)
- [Practice] Lone Star Legal Aid's three-chatbot project reported in February 2026 that most of the work was non-technical: cross-department coordination, internal user testing, security planning, governance and accuracy safeguards. — [Lone Star Legal Aid, Feb 2026](https://www.lonestarlegal.org/news/2026/02/building-smarter-access-to-justice-an-update-on-lone-star-legal-aids-ai-powered-chatbot-project/)
- [Practice] Legal-chatbot QA: the quality of automated evaluation depended almost entirely on a human-designed evaluation framework, which points to human-in-the-loop evaluation. — [Siren Analytics](https://www.sirenanalytics.com/main-content/human-vs.-automated-quality-assurance-for-legal-ai-lessons-from-evaluating-a-legal-chatbot)
- [Claim] A Finnish LLM legal-aid chatbot prototype (master's thesis) concludes that responsible AI legal information is achievable if accuracy, privacy and scope limits are non-negotiable design constraints from the outset. — [Turku AMK](https://talkbystudents.turkuamk.fi/master-school-business/can-ai-help-ordinary-people-access-legal-information-lessons-from-designing-an-llm-assisted-legal-aid-chatbot/)
- [Evidence/Practice] Stanford user research found a tension between formal legal accuracy and the empathy and clarity users want. A January 2024 interview study on AI for legal problems recommended algorithmic literacy, better legal-advice warnings and accessibility improvements. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab AI-A2J](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/category/current-projects/ai-a2j/page/2); [class report](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/projects/ai-access-to-justice/class-report)
- [Claim] Stanford's 2026 "AI for Legal Help" class report covers scoping, building and testing legal-aid tech, including where AI does and does not fit. I only saw the table of contents. — [Stanford report PDF](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Scoping-Building-and-Testing-New-Legal-Aid-Tech-Systems_Report-Compress.pdf)
- [Evidence] Inheritance-specific citizen information is mostly curated, non-AI portals. CNUE's "Successions in Europe" covers succession law in 22 EU member states and is promoted as a tool to consult before visiting a notary. The EU e-Justice portal links to it and hosts **EU Adapt**, a practitioner tool for finding the "closest equivalent right in rem" across jurisdictions. Your Europe explains that heirs may settle an estate with the courts of the deceased's last habitual residence or with notaries. — [Notaries of Europe](https://notariesofeurope.eu/en/citizens/successions-in-europe); [e-Justice succession](https://e-justice.europa.eu/166/EN/succession); [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/family/inheritances/managing-inheritance/index_ga.htm)
- [Claim] UK firm Manak Solicitors launched a 2026 "Legalese Translator" that rewrites documents in plain English, including Gen Z or "sassy" tones. It is framed as "simplified explanations", not "automated legal advice", and has no published evaluation. — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/law-firms-ai-tool-translates-legalese-into-gen-z-or-sassy-english)

### Inferences
- Positioning: "explains your letter and helps you prepare for your notary or lawyer", never "replaces your lawyer". Claims of accuracy need test evidence (DoNotPay precedent). A "questions to ask your notary" export is a natural, low-risk output.
- EU Adapt's "closest equivalent" concept mapping and CNUE's 22-country succession database are candidate seed sources for a jurisdiction-agnostic inheritance ontology with local-term mappings, subject to licensing.
- Evaluate with expert-designed rubrics (Stanford/Beagle+ model) covering actionability, empowerment, avoiding needless fear, correctness and referral quality, plus human review of a sample. LLM-as-judge alone is not enough.

### Gaps
- Courtroom5 did not appear in my searches. I have no sourced findings on its design or outcomes.
- I did not verify UK government initiatives (e.g. GOV.UK Chat), the Citizens Advice "Caddy" pilot details (mentioned in search output without a verified URL), or EU member-state government legal assistants.
- I found no notary-association AI assistant for inheritance. This is absence of evidence from limited searching, not proof that none exists. Searches of national notary chambers (e.g. Germany's BNotK, France's Notaires de France, Austria's ÖNK) are recommended.

## 5. Emotional / bereavement context: UX for grieving users, cognitive load, tone

### Takeaway
Guidance for bereaved users consistently recommends plain, non-euphemistic language, fewer steps and gentle triage, no upsells, and not presuming how the user feels. The peer-reviewed evidence base is thin and mostly qualitative. Simplification measurably reduces perceived task load (the 0.33/5 NASA-TLX gain in section 3), which supports load-reduction design for this audience.

### Cited Findings
- [Practice] UK DWP Digital/GDS guidance on content for people dealing with a death: use plain language rather than euphemisms, which are harder to understand, especially for non-native speakers. Do not assume every user is grieving, and do not tell users how they should feel. — [DWP Digital blog](https://dwpdigital.blog.gov.uk/2020/02/06/designing-content-for-people-dealing-with-a-death/)
- [Evidence, qualitative] A 2026 Design for Health study of a prototype digital bereavement service identified success factors including emotionally sensitive language, streamlined triage and reduced cognitive load. I saw only a partial list. — [University of Suffolk repository](https://oars.uos.ac.uk/5647/1/Integrating%20an%20ethic%20of%20care%20into%20the%20design%20of%20digital%20bereavement%20services---.pdf)
- [Practice] A local-government bereavement service redesign found that the process spans government and private providers, and that staff struggled as well as residents. — [Contra case study](https://contra.com/p/OkbUke3N-redesigning-the-bereavement-service-for-a-local-government-body)
- [Practice] Post-mortem account management research: thoughtful friction and transparency matter, but friction must not become a burden. Heavily automated flows were disliked. — [Built In](https://builtin.com/design-ux/post-mortem-account-management)
- [Claim] "Thanatosensitive design" argues that most technologies do not account for users' deaths, which complicates matters for bereaved families. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/research/publication/thanatosensitively-designed-technologies-for-bereavement-support-2/)
- [Practice] Memorial-platform design guidance: clear pathways, minimal distractions, gentle prompts and no upsells in vulnerable moments. — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/amelia_brown04/designing-meaningful-digital-memorials-after-cat-cremation-2g8i)
- [Evidence] Stanford's rubric includes "avoids needless fear or discouragement" as a quality criterion, and user testing found users value empathy and clarity alongside accuracy. — [Stanford Legal Design Lab](https://justiceinnovation.law.stanford.edu/tag/benchmarks/)

### Inferences
- Tone: one short, sincere acknowledgement at the start ("I'm sorry about your father"), then factual and direct. No repeated condolences and no assumptions about feelings. Use literal terms ("died", "estate"); explain legal euphemisms and jargon rather than adding softer ones.
- Reduce cognitive load: lead with "Do you need to do anything right now? Yes/No + date". Use progressive disclosure for diagrams and taxonomy ("Explore the concepts" is optional). Save progress, allow "remind me later", and offer printable or shareable summaries for siblings or co-heirs.
- Triage urgency: separate time-critical actions (renunciation deadlines, which in several civil-law systems are short) from "can wait" items. Respect that grief can delay engagement by offering calendar reminders, informed by the evidence that text reminders reduce missed obligations.
- Multi-party sensitivity: who-is-who diagrams may reveal family conflict or unknown heirs. Use neutral labels and avoid inferring relationships the letter does not state.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of grieving users interacting with legal or administrative AI assistants, or of whether diagrams help or overwhelm users under grief-related cognitive load.
- The bereavement UX sources are largely practitioner blogs plus one qualitative academic paper. Effect sizes are unavailable.
