## 2. Core concept: knowledge graphs, glossaries, ontologies and a Reviewer AI

**User:**

> Let's start working on an AI assistant / expert that can help users with their questions related to business/technical domains
> as close to a real human expert as possible.
>
> User-journey #1: download user manuals of business/domain
> The "domain admin" user
>  - uses the  "downloader" application to downloads user manuals for his/her business / technical domain
> The downloader application
>  - processes the downloaded user manuals: create indexes, build knowledge graph, glossary, ontology, ... needed for answering questions
>  - collects quality issues: gaps, contradictions, terminology drift, ...
>
> User journey #2: answering questions
> The "user"
>  - asks questions about a business / technical domain from the "UserManualAndDomain AI Assistant" application
> The "UserManualAndDomain AI Assistant" application
>  - answers the questions from one or more downloaded user manuals and their related indexes, knowledge graphs, glossaries, ontologies, ...
>  - presents quality issues: contradictions, terminology drift, gaps, ... within and across the user manuals
> If there is a quality issue with the user manual(s) the "user" should know about it
>
> User journey #3: review, governance and quality resolution:
> The "domain admin" user starts the "reviewer" application to resolve the quality issues and prepare higher-level concepts needed for answering the questions better.
> The "Reviewer AI assistant" presents the
>  - the extracted terms, concepts, relations
>  - the proposed higher-level concepts, abstractions, ...
>  - the proposed quality issues: gaps, contradictions, terminology drifts, ...
> The "Reviewer AI assistant" pguides the "domain admin user" interactively to
>  - build and align the concepts into a coherent, consistent, complete, compact busines domain
> NOTE: The "Reviewer AI Assistant"
>  - proactively drives the review process
>  - presents the quality issues visually
>  - presents the proposed higher-level concepts, relations and properties of both to the "domain admin" user for review
>  - uses diagrams to present taxonomies, ontologies, knowledge graphs, ... in an easy-to-understand form
> The "domain admin" adds corrections or approves the proposals.
>
> Question: do you think that knowledge graphs, glossaries and ontologies will help us to achieve those goals?
> Do you think that a "Reviewer AI assistant" can conduct an interactive review/governance process?

**Assistant:**

Yes to both questions. Glossaries, ontologies and knowledge graphs will help, but not mainly by making answers more accurate. A Reviewer AI assistant can run the interactive review, though nobody has yet published a tested, complete version of one.

**Will glossaries, ontologies and knowledge graphs help?**
- **Glossary:** clearly helps every journey. It maps synonyms and abbreviations in user questions to the manuals' terms, provides definitions, and is the basis for detecting terminology drift.
- **Ontology:** helps when it is a small core schema owned by people. It lets the assistant scope answers by product, version and role, and ask clarifying questions. Fully automatic ontology building is weak: building the concept hierarchy scores only about 0.12–0.66 on the standard F1 accuracy measure.
- **Knowledge graph:** improves answers only a little, and only for multi-hop, comparison and time-ordering questions. Graph retrieval loses on the simple factual, procedural and definitional questions that make up most manual Q&A. When graph summaries drive the answer, it also gets "this isn't documented" right much less often (about 14% vs 91% with plain search). Where the graph really pays off is as the backbone for finding quality issues and structuring the review.
- **Quality issues:**
  - Rule-based checks find undefined terms, structural gaps and deprecated terms well.
  - An LLM alone finds only about 64% of contradictions, and about 31% of what it flags is noise. A better approach is to extract facts tagged with product and version, then compare them with rules.
  - Users should see conflicts from a pre-computed issue list, because LLMs don't reliably notice them while answering.

**Can a Reviewer AI assistant drive the review?**
- Every part of it already exists somewhere: research prototypes, and products such as TopBraid EDG's problem-and-suggestion panel and PoolParty's accept/reject suggestions.
- The main risks are human, not technical:
  - Reviewers tend to approve wrong suggestions.
  - The assistant tends to agree with the admin's corrections even when they are wrong.
  - Fatigue sets in after roughly 100 items.
- The report's design advice:
  - Store the review queue and its state in a database, not in the conversation.
  - Show the source excerpt behind every proposal.
  - Review the highest-impact, least-certain items first.
  - For key items, have the admin decide before seeing the AI's opinion.
  - Require a second approver for merges and deletions.
  - Turn approved decisions into regression tests for later extraction runs.

**Suggested build order:** glossary, a graph of the manuals' own structure (contents, numbered steps, menu paths, cross-references), combined semantic-plus-keyword search, rule-based issue detectors, and a way to measure answer quality. Add a full GraphRAG-style index later, and only if measurements show you need it.

No public benchmark uses user manuals, and several of the figures are self-reported or weren't checked against the full papers. Testing on your own manuals early will tell you more than the published numbers can.

## 3. Emerging domains, cross-domain understanding, reasoning, evolution, experimentation

**User:**

> Additional goals:
>  - support user manuals from very new business / technical domains, where the concepts are not well-known yet or in a forming phase. e.g.: AI, quantum computers,
>  - support overlapping business / technical domains: e.g.: medical diagnosis & computer science. The application should be able to answer medical questions of engineers and engineering questions of medical experts. Their primary pain point is that they don't know the concepts and the relations between the concepts.
>  - support understanding concepts of a new business domain. e.g.: there are many different kinds of lawers, judges, other roles and concepts in legal processes. For an average engineer it is a completely new area. The taxonomy tree of lawyers, the ontology graph of a court trial will speed up generic understanding.
>  - The AI assistant will be able to make deductions using the taxonomy, ontology, knowledge graph and answer questions better than a typical keywork/RAG search engine.
>  - The application shall be able to answer the conceptual questions based on glossary, knowledge graph, ontology, ...
>  - the glossary and ontology should be able to evolve as the understanding changes
>  - experiment to find the right combination of technologies
>
> Do you agree that using glossaries, taxonomies, ontologies will help to achieve those extra goals?

**Assistant:**

Yes, I agree, and the evidence supports it for all seven goals. These structures help most where the language model knows least: brand-new concepts, contested definitions, and concepts outside the user's own field. Where the support is strong and where it's conditional:

| Goal | Verdict | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Very new domains (AI, quantum) | **Yes, conditionally**: only if a concept can hold several definitions, each with its source, date, scope and a status (proposed / contested / accepted). One "official" definition per term won't work while a field is still forming. | Models handle brand-new terms badly: new words roughly halve translation quality, and models invent definitions for fake terms. Standards lag the field by years. Published AI ontologies are still unstable. |
| 2. Overlapping domains (medicine × CS) | **Strong yes**: one shared concept with each field's own name for it, and links labeled exact / close / related / analogy. | Medical terminology infrastructure is mature (UMLS: about 3.5M concepts). Matching concepts across ontologies with an LLM alone is unreliable, so a classical matcher that asks the LLM only about uncertain pairs works best. No benchmark exists that matches medical concepts to computer-science ones. |
| 3. Learning a new domain fast (legal roles) | **Strong yes**, and the best-evidenced goal. Show a tree first and expand to the full graph only on request. | Meta-analyses of concept maps show moderate learning gains: g≈0.43 for studying a map, 0.72 for building one. Tree-shaped maps take less effort to read than network graphs. Legal role taxonomies have to differ by jurisdiction (common law vs civil law). |
| 4. Deductions | **Yes, but narrower than intuition suggests**: graph queries or a reasoner must compute the inference, and the LLM only explains it. | In one enterprise benchmark, accuracy rose from 16% to 54% when the same data was queried as a knowledge graph instead of SQL. But LLMs are weak at ontology reasoning themselves, and generating graph queries from free text is only about 30–60% accurate. Reviewed, reusable query templates are the safer pattern. |
| 5. Conceptual questions | **Split by question type**: "what is X" needs only the glossary. "Kinds of X", "how X relates to Y", "difference between" and "prerequisites" are where graphs gain roughly 8–13 points. | Moderate evidence, from two benchmarks. |
| 6. Glossary and ontology evolving over time | **Yes, if built in from day one**: concept IDs never change, a concept whose meaning changes is retired and replaced rather than edited, and every answer records which vocabulary release it used. | The OWL, SKOS, OBO and PROV standards are mature. Fully automated, LLM-driven evolution is still immature, so changes go through the Reviewer. |
| 7. Experimenting with technology combinations | **Essential**, because the best stack changes with the question type. | The report includes a phased plan: baselines, screening components, end-to-end comparison on a human-written test set, robustness tests, and a study with real newcomers. Results are reported per question type, with error bars. |

The report also adds to the earlier architecture:
- definitions stored as separate sourced records with a status lifecycle;
- a mapping layer between domains;
- a profile of which concepts each user already knows, linked to prerequisites;
- a question router that sends each question type to its own retrieval method;
- new review queues for the Reviewer.

Two of the proposed experiments would be new results in their own right, because no published study covers them:
- whether concept maps and analogies help adult newcomers learn faster with an LLM assistant;
- whether ontology-routed answering beats RAG on concepts newer than the model's training cutoff.

Some figures are self-reported or come from a single preprint. The report flags each of these, and I'd treat them as directional.

## 4. Internal enterprise platform domains

**User:**

> Another use-case: internal/new business/technical domains in a large enterprise.
>  - platform temas expose many services, applications, resources, capabilities
>  - platform services, organization, use-cases evolve exremely fast
>  - product teams and new adopting teams need to learn a relatively complex technical domain
>  - platform services are built on each other, use other platform services or overlapping
>  - user teams (a.k.a. product teams)  are overloaded by the high cognitive load caused by new techical terms to achieve their end-to-end goals
>  - user manuals are often outdated, overlapping, contradicting to each other, ...
>
> Goal:
>  - Let's benefit from LLMs, AI assistants, glossaries, knowledge graphs, ontologies, ...
>  - so that product teams can ask questions and get answers (rather than request personal consultation sessions)
>  - platform teams can align their user manuals to each other to provide a better end-to-end user experience for the product teams
>  - provide tools for the platform teams to create high-quality services and concepts and be able to create glossaries, taxonomies, ontologies, knowledge graphs in a semi-automated way.
>  - reduce the amount of personal consultation needed
>
> Specific features:
>  - ability to see the taxonomy of different kinds of platform resources that can be created in a platform visually
>  - ability to see the ontology of platform resources visually e.g.:  compound platform resources with multiple components
>  - ability to see the relations between the different kinds of relations between the platform, services, platform teams, kinds of platform resources, ... visually
>  - reduce the confusing synonyms
>  - use all those visualizations for the platform teams to govern their glossary, taxonomy, ontology, knowledge graph and detect quality issues in their user manuals
>  - use the visualizations to explain concepts to product teams efficiently (nobody has time any more to read long-long pages)
>  - store all the content in standard formats: OWL, RDF, SCHL, Turtle, JSON-LD, SHACL, ...
>  - add tooling support and process for the product teams to build their glossary, taxonomies, ontologies, knowledge graphs, ...,

**Assistant:**

Yes, the approach fits internal platform domains, and it fits them better than customer manuals. Most of the structure already exists in machine-readable form: the developer-portal catalog (Backstage or Port), Kubernetes CRDs, Crossplane compositions, Terraform provider schemas and OpenAPI specs. So you can mostly harvest and curate the glossary, taxonomy, ontology and knowledge graph instead of extracting them from prose.

**What's missing today.** No portal provides the layer your problems need:
- one preferred term per concept, with its synonyms;
- a class hierarchy of resource kinds;
- the parts that make up compound resources;
- a split between capabilities and the services that provide them, which makes overlapping services visible with a simple query;
- a record of where each fact came from;
- links from doc pages to the things they describe.

**Verdicts on your goals and features:**
- **Strong yes:** the visual taxonomy of resource kinds, reducing synonyms, storing everything in standard formats, and generating the schema layer from the existing machine-readable sources.
- **Yes, with moderate evidence:**
  - the views of compound resources and of relations between platforms, services and teams;
  - product teams asking the assistant instead of booking consultations. Companies report real relief, but the numbers are their own: Uber's assistant was rated helpful 48.9% of the time, GoDaddy's handles over 80% of platform requests alone, and Spotify reports 47% faster support resolution.
- **Weak evidence, though plausible:**
  - aligning manuals across platform teams;
  - measurably fewer consultations: no independent study exists. Plan around Elastic's 7% drop in support cases, not vendor claims of 70–95%.
  - using the same visuals both for governance and for explaining concepts;
  - product teams building their own models.

**What the report gives you:**
- **Architecture:** an RDF-based semantic layer next to your existing portal, which stays in place. This departs from the first report's property-graph default, because here the sources are already structured.
- **Data model:** reuses standard vocabularies (ORG, DCAT 3, SKOS, a W3C part-whole pattern, PROV-O, Backstage relation names), with a short Turtle example.
- **Toolchain:**
  - Small harvester programs open pull requests that propose new resource kinds and terms.
  - LinkML is the single schema source.
  - CI checks every change with ROBOT, pySHACL and voc4cat.
  - Doc-linting rules (Vale) are generated from the glossary.
  - Product teams get a template repo to start their own glossary.
- **Visualization:** five view types: resource-kind tree, compound resource, relations around one concept, capability-overlap matrix, and synonym cluster.
  - Each has a simple version for product teams and a quality-issue overlay for platform teams.
  - Diagrams are generated by code from the graph, not drawn by the LLM, and kept to about 15–30 nodes.
  - A short "concept card" format is included for answers in chat.
- **Governance:** platform teams own their own modules, and a small central group owns shared terms. The pieces are a semantic council, a terminology steward per platform, a developer-experience team, named owners for journeys that span platforms, and a Reviewer queue per platform.
- **Metrics and rollout:** fewer consultations is measured by comparing onboarded teams against teams not yet onboarded. Rollout is phased: baseline, then the core model, a pilot, scale-up and tuning, each with exit criteria.

**Gaps in the evidence:**
- No published case study shows a platform ontology governed this way.
- No ready-made converter exists from Backstage, CRDs or Terraform schemas to RDF/OWL, so expect to write small extractors.
- No study tests concept cards in chat.
- No study measures how much the knowledge graph adds on top of search plus the catalog API. That's the first thing worth measuring in a pilot.

## 5. Change management in git

**User:**

> Another goals: change management
>  - glossary, taxonomies, ontologies, knowledge graphs, rules, ... are stored in standard formats: OWL, RDF, turtles, SCHAL, ... in a version-control system like git
>  - the governance process keeps previous states and changes are introduced as versions

**Assistant:**

Both goals are achievable for the content your teams decide on: glossary, taxonomy, ontology, SHACL rules, mappings and hand-curated graph facts. Git can be the system of record for all of it, with governed releases that keep every previous state. The evidence is strong: the FIBO finance ontology and the OBO biomedical ontologies have both worked this way for years with actively maintained tools.

**Three limits shape the design:**
1. **Harvested instance data doesn't belong in git.** Data from Backstage, CRDs and Terraform changes daily. Each harvest run instead goes into its own named graph, with a record of where it came from and a diff against the previous run. A run becomes "current" only after it passes SHACL validation and a check for unusually large deletions, since those usually mean the harvester failed. Changes that need a new class or term become automatic pull requests for review; everything else is just logged. The rule is: content you decide goes in git, content you observe goes in the store.
2. **No maintained tool can merge RDF changes from two teams by meaning.** The recommendation is to prevent conflicts rather than resolve them:
   - every file is rewritten in one fixed order on each commit, using a pinned formatter (EDM Council rdf-toolkit or Cool RDF), so diffs stay readable;
   - each team owns its own modules, enforced through CODEOWNERS;
   - CI requires the other owner's review when two branches change the same terms;
   - a merge queue re-validates the combined result before it lands.
3. **There is no published rule for what counts as a "breaking" change** in a glossary, SHACL rules or mappings. The report proposes a matrix per artifact type, but that is our own design, not a standard. For example, tightening a SHACL rule breaks whoever produces the data, and loosening one breaks whoever relies on the guarantee.

**Recommended setup:**
- **Versioning:** one version number covers everything released together, using major.minor.patch. CI works out the minimum change class from the diff, and the Reviewer AI can raise that class but never lower it. Terms marked "provisional" aren't covered by the compatibility promise.
- **Deprecation:** never delete or reuse a term's identifier. A retired term keeps its definition and points to its successor, and its old name stays searchable on the successor. It leaves the active views no earlier than the next major release plus an announcement window of about 90 days.
- **Releases:** follow the FIBO/OBO pattern:
  - stable identifiers for terms, plus a versioned identifier for each release;
  - release bundles that never change after publication;
  - persistent web addresses that redirect to the right version;
  - a changelog generated from structured change records (KGCL).
- **Governance:** approval thresholds depend on the change class. For major changes, the human records their own classification before seeing the AI's.
- **"As of" answers:** each release is loaded into its own named graph. Every answer, validation report and quality issue records the ontology release, the git commit and the data snapshot it used. Recording only the ontology version isn't enough to reproduce an answer. No published system stamps LLM answers this way, so this part would be new.
- **Tools to avoid:** turtle-formatter, Ecco, Blazegraph, Quit Store, R43ples, OSTRICH and Stardog's built-in versioning, which have been archived, dropped or show no recent releases. The rdf-delta server says it will be archived. TerminusDB and ConVer-G are worth watching but not adopting yet.

The implementation steps fit into the rollout phases from the enterprise-platform report rather than adding new ones. The approval thresholds, the 90-day window and the breaking-change matrix are design proposals, not established standards. We also couldn't confirm whether RDF 1.2 is now a final W3C Recommendation, or whether the GraphDB history plugin is still current.

## 6. Hypothetical consumer use case: understanding an inheritance letter

**User:**

> Another (hypothetical) use-case:
>  - My father passed away, I get an official/legal email from a lawyer/court about the inheritance duties
>  - I cannot understand the email properly, I have no understanding of the global legal system
>  - the application with a legal glossary, taxonomy, ontology, knowledge graph helps me by
>     - answering my clarification questions about my participation, other participants, duties, who-is-who, ...
>     - I can read diagrams visually and learn quickly: taxonomy diagram, ontology graph
>  - the application shows the related parts from the legal business domain:
>     - definitions of the terms mentioned in the email
>     - ontology diagram of the concepts, terms mentioned in the email and their relations
>     - possible reated terms I need to know about in case of inheritance process

**Assistant (clarifying question):** **[deep-research · clarifying question]** Inheritance procedures, the people involved (notary, probate court, executor, heirs) and their duties differ a lot between legal systems. Which jurisdiction(s) should the research on the inheritance use case focus on? (Hungary / Germany & EU civil law / US & UK common law / jurisdiction-agnostic)

**User:** This is just a hypothetical use-case to position the goal of this research and application. NOTE: we are in the brainstorming phase.

**Assistant:**

Yes, the app can help in this scenario, with one firm boundary: it can explain what the letter says, but it must not tell the user what to decide. The report treats this as brainstorming, not legal advice, and stays jurisdiction-agnostic.

**Verdict per capability:**
- **Explaining the email (definitions, a diagram of the concepts it mentions, related terms): the strongest case.**
  - **Definitions:** strong yes. They come from a curated glossary for that country's law and keep the original-language term.
  - **Concept diagram:** works if it stays small, around 7 concepts, with the user at the centre.
  - **"Related terms you'll need":** works if they come from a curated model of the inheritance process rather than from the LLM.
  - **Evidence:** no one has tested the diagram or related-terms ideas with users. The nearest evidence isn't about AI: New York's redesigned court summons cut missed court dates by about 13%.
- **Clarification questions (my role, who-is-who, duties): conditional yes.**
  - **What the letter states:** strong yes, with every fact highlighted in the original text.
  - **General duties and next steps:** only from a curated country module, and only after the user confirms which country's law applies. Even professional legal research tools still make errors on 17–33% of queries.
  - **"Should I accept, disclaim, sign or pay?":** the app doesn't answer. It shows the options, their general consequences, and who to ask.
- **Learning through diagrams:**
  - Timelines and role diagrams have moderate evidence from controlled experiments, though those tested professionals and civil servants, not laypeople.
  - A taxonomy tree fits as an optional "explore" panel.
  - A full ontology graph is a tool for experts and curators, not a first view for a grieving user.

**Reference design:**
- **Model:** a shared core of about 60–150 concepts. Roles are defined by what they do, for example "the person who manages the estate".
  - Each country module keeps its native terms and links them to the core with typed relations, never as exact equivalents.
  - "False friends" are flagged, for example the English "executor" and the German "Testamentsvollstrecker" are not the same role.
  - Each module has a switch for how the estate passes: automatically to the heirs at death (Hungary, Germany), or through a court-appointed representative (England).
- **Deadlines:** stored as structured records, never generated. Each records who must act, what starts the clock, how long it runs, what happens if it's missed, and the legal source.
- **Screen:** short acknowledgement of the death → "Do I need to act now?" → an action card (what, by when, where) → a plain-language version shown beside the original → definitions on tap → a who-is-who diagram that shows only relationships the letter states. A "questions to ask your notary or lawyer" sheet is the safest high-value output.
- **Seed sources:** the EU certificate-of-succession form, the EU e-Justice country fact sheets, the notaries' 22-country succession database, and the LKIF Core and FOLIO legal ontologies.

**Constraints:**
- **Information vs advice:** the line has to hold. In the US, unauthorized-practice-of-law rules now reach even free advice, and the FTC fined DoNotPay over its "robot lawyer" claims.
- **EU AI Act:** probably only the duty to tell users they're talking to an AI. A version sold to notaries could count as high-risk.
- **GDPR:** the heavier everyday burden, because the letter names living co-heirs. That means redacting personal data before it reaches the model and not training on uploads.
- **Liability:** the new EU Product Liability Directive applies from 9 December 2026, and disclaimers protect little, so the safeguards have to be built into the design.

**Lessons for the general product:**
- One domain with several variants (jurisdictions here, product editions elsewhere) uses the same pattern: a shared core plus a module per variant.
- The user's own document is a main way in.
- Every claim is labeled as one of three kinds: stated in your document, generally true for this variant, or depends on facts the app can't check.
- Values that cause harm if wrong, such as deadlines, version numbers or limits, are never generated.
- The question router needs a "decision" type that gives options instead of a recommendation.
- Diagrams are chosen by task: action views for users, trees for learners, full graphs for curators.

**Not verified:** the Hungarian deadline for disclaiming an inheritance, a reported six-month variant in Germany, and whether the AI Act changes have been formally published. US probate law wasn't researched. The report proposes eight experiments. The most important is an A/B test of whether the diagrams help grieving users understand their letter.
